# Wrap official glyphs, print everything else

Board recognizes pieces with a closed-set ML classifier, so a hobbyist family cannot create genuinely new recognized pieces today. You can use only the official Piece Sets and their prebuilt models. You can still build very creative pieces by wrapping official pieces in 3D-printed toppers, collars, turntables, frames and housings, and by turning their reported identity, position, roughly 1° rotation, touched state and lift/place phase into game controls ([Board FAQ](https://docs.dev.board.fun/faq); [Docs: Pieces](https://docs.dev.board.fun/learn/pieces)). The best starting point is **Board Bases**, six generic glyph bases made for 25mm minis with their own public model (board_bases v0.3.2). They are the official "attach your own figure" pattern and fit printed toppers naturally ([Board Tabletop](https://board.fun/products/board-tabletop); [model manifest](https://dev.board.fun/downloads/models/manifest.json)). Among shipped games, only a few use pieces in a mechanically inventive way. **Spycraft** is the standout, with a modular 7-piece Spy Kit (dial, slider, frames and inserts) assembled into different tools. Most other games map one piece to one tool or character. That leaves plenty of room for what the family is planning. Truly custom conductive gadgets (DIY finger-like pads, stamps, buttons) are a secondary, experimental track. They will probably register only while a hand is touching them, and Board's palm filtering might remove them. Nothing like this has been tested on Board, so every DIY-conductive idea below needs a hardware test when the console arrives.

## Spycraft's dial kit is the lone mechanical standout in about 23 games

The official library now lists about 23 entries: a bundled Arcade family (ships and robots), Chop Chop, and roughly 15 paid titles at about $35, each with its own piece set ([Board games page](https://board.fun/pages/games)). Most games use pieces well but literally. Chop Chop's cleaver chops, its pepper mill twists, and its shared spoon creates what one reviewer called "real-world body choreography." Mushka has a watering can, brush, hair dryer and bubble wand, plus a "wind-up" carousel. Omakase uses a chopstick controller that moves in straight lines, and Starfire reloads when you sweep a ship over pellets ([Wargamer review](https://www.wargamer.com/board/games-console-review); [Co-Optimus review](https://www.co-optimus.com/blog/article-poster/3279/board-review-2026-does-the-tabletop-console-deliver-on-its-social-play-promise.html)). Three designs go further. **The Heist** treats piece rotation as an optical angle: up to six mirrors and two splitters redirect on-screen laser tripwires ([Meeple Mountain Summer 2026](https://www.meeplemountain.com/articles/the-quarterly-board-meeting-summer-2026/)). **Strata** stands and stacks 3D pentomino blocks that are deliberately non-capacitive and "never report touched" ([Docs: Piece Interaction Design](https://docs.dev.board.fun/guides/piece-interaction-design)). **Spycraft** is the most inventive.

The spy game with dials is **Spycraft** by State of Play, not The Heist. Its 7-piece Spy Kit combines "a dial, a slider, squares with cutouts for circular components" into a screwdriver, codebreaker, safe cracker, magnifier or X-ray ([Meeple Mountain review](https://www.meeplemountain.com/reviews/board-fun-device/); [Wargamer preview](https://www.wargamer.com/board/game-console-preview)). The support docs give the main clue to how it works. "All pieces (including the black inner pieces) must lie completely flat on the display," and white frames must not be upside down ([Spycraft FAQs](https://board.gorgias.help/en-US/spycraft-faqs-4317832)). **Unverified inference:** each inner disc or slider insert is probably its own glyph touching the glass inside a frame's cutout. The game would then read the insert's rotation or position relative to the frame, not any internal mechanism. The docs never mention multi-state pieces, and their design guide recommends rotation for "dials and combination locks" ([Docs: Piece Interaction Design](https://docs.dev.board.fun/guides/piece-interaction-design)). That pattern is the most important thing to copy, because it creates mechanical-feeling controls from nothing but glyph position and angle. One limitation: Spycraft's model does not appear in the public developer manifest, so we probably cannot build with the Spy Kit itself (inference from the [manifest](https://dev.board.fun/downloads/models/manifest.json)).

## The SDK lets you interpret contacts but not author pieces

Every contact arrives at about 60 fps and carries a contact ID, x/y at 1920×1080, orientation (about 1°, glyphs only), contact type (Finger, Glyph, or an undocumented "Blob"), glyph ID, an isTouched flag, and a phase (began, moved, stationary, ended, canceled) ([Docs: Pieces](https://docs.dev.board.fun/learn/pieces); [Web SDK on npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@board.fun/web-sdk)). Developers choose the Piece Set Model (.tflite). In Unity they can also tune translation smoothing, rotation smoothing and persistence (how long a lifted piece stays tracked), and they can switch settings assets at runtime ([Unity Setup Reference](https://docs.dev.board.fun/unity/getting-started/setup-reference)). Runtime switching matters for a family "lab" app, since each mini-game can load a different set. Everything else, including twist, shake, hold-to-confirm and lift-and-stamp, is game logic built on those snapshots. Only one Piece Set can be active at a time. The exceptions are prebuilt combined models: **Chop Chop + Mushka** and **Thrasos + Arcade** ([Docs: Pieces](https://docs.dev.board.fun/learn/pieces)). The public manifest lists ten models: Arcade, Board Bases, Chop Chop, Heist, Mushka, Omakase, Save the Bloogs, Thrasos and those two combos ([manifest](https://dev.board.fun/downloads/models/manifest.json)). Glyph IDs are unnamed integers. To find each ID, place pieces one at a time and log them ([Touch guide](https://docs.dev.board.fun/guides/touch-input)).

On new pieces, the FAQ is explicit: "currently you can only use existing Pieces and models." Custom piece sets come only through a game submission manufactured in partnership with Board, or through the announced AI-driven **Board Studio**, described as "planned for later this year." Its launch status as of November 2026 is unconfirmed ([Board FAQ](https://docs.dev.board.fun/faq); [Pulse 2.0](https://pulse2.com/board-raises-20-million-series-a-to-build-the-future-of-face-to-face-gaming-and-creation/)). At launch, a Board engineer said homemade multi-material printed pieces are "possible" but "pretty finicky" ([HN thread](https://hn.svelte.dev/item/45742456)). Because the classifier only outputs IDs it was trained on, a homemade glyph could at best clone an existing piece; it cannot be a new identity. The license says the SDK is "designed exclusively to be run on Board through the use of our existing pieces sets" and bars reverse engineering and derivative works, so training your own model is off the table ([Developer Terms](https://docs.dev.board.fun/more/license)). The undocumented **Blob** type ("neither a recognized finger nor a glyph") hints that unclassified conductive objects may surface as anonymous position-only contacts. Its size limits, accuracy and filtering are undocumented, so treat it as a test item, not a design foundation ([Web SDK types](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@board.fun/web-sdk)).

### What we can and can't do

| Goal | Verdict | How |
|---|---|---|
| Use official pieces in our own game with new meanings | **Yes** | Load a public model and keep our own glyph-ID name table ([Web SDK](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@board.fun/web-sdk)) |
| Printed toppers, shells, collars, frames around official pieces | **Yes (design inference, well grounded)** | Board Bases are the official precedent ([Board Tabletop](https://board.fun/products/board-tabletop)) |
| Dials, sliders, selectors and buttons made from official glyph motion | **Yes, untested on Board** | Rotation, relative position, lift/place phase |
| Touch-to-activate through a printed shell | **Probably, if a conductive path exists** | Copper tape or conductive PLA from grip to the official piece's body |
| A new recognized glyph identity | **No** | Closed-set model; the license limits use to existing sets |
| A cloned glyph of an existing piece | **Possible but "finicky," legally grey** | Multi-material printing ([HN](https://hn.svelte.dev/item/45742456)) |
| DIY conductive pads read as Finger/Blob contacts | **Unknown, likely touched-only** | Needs hardware testing |
| Use the Spy Kit or Strata/Sports/Fall-collection sets | **Probably not** | Not in the public manifest |
| Sell toppers | **Not addressed by Terms** | Avoid Board branding; ask hello@board.fun first |

## Physics sets the limits: the base must touch glass, and shells kill touch

Capacitive tangible research explains the practical rules. A passive conductor resting on the screen is weak. A hand supplies the ground coupling that makes it read like a fingertip, which is the same physics behind Board's isTouched flag. Screens also filter out static passive objects over time through adaptive baselines ([PERCs](https://hci.rwth-aachen.de/percs); [Clip-on Gadgets](https://core.ac.uk/download/357520689.pdf)). Insulation is the enemy. In a systematic 3D-printed marker study, untouched markers with bare conductive pads were detected 100% of the time on iPad but only about **20% with 0.56 mm of plastic between pad and glass**. **10 mm pads significantly beat 5 mm pads**, and bridges thinner than 1 mm merged into a single touch ([MDPI Inventions 2018](https://www.mdpi.com/2411-5134/3/1/9)). Official Board pieces solve the untouched problem with engineered glyphs and on-device ML. DIY pads do not get that benefit, and Board's spacing, pad size and palm-filter thresholds are unpublished.

These findings lead to four rules for every design below. First, **the official base's bottom must stay uncovered and flat on the glass**: no felt, tape or printed skin under it, and no tilting. Tracking also drops when a piece moves fast, lifts at an angle or is covered by a hand ([Docs: Piece Interaction Design](https://docs.dev.board.fun/guides/piece-interaction-design)). Second, **a non-conductive PLA/PETG shell will likely block isTouched** unless it leaves the official body exposed or adds a low-resistance path. Copper tape is the most reliable; Proto-pasta conductive PLA measures about 30 Ω·cm printed in XY, a few kΩ per 10 cm, which is enough for touch ([Proto-pasta](https://proto-pasta.com/pages/conductive-pla)). Third, **DIY conductive pads will probably appear only while held**. They need about 10 mm feet, 15–20 mm center spacing and bare conductor at the glass, and they sit in an unmeasured window between "too small to detect" and "so large Board filters it as a palm." Palm and forearm rejection happens "at the hardware level" ([Touch System](https://docs.dev.board.fun/learn/touch-system)). Fourth, **windows and light pipes only work outside the base footprint**. The game knows each glyph's position and angle (about 92.6 px per inch on the 23.8" panel), so it can draw content under an offset window that follows the piece ([Docs: Hardware](https://docs.dev.board.fun/learn/hardware)). Clear resin gives a true lens; FDM PETG is only translucent (inference). Stacking official pieces probably fails, since only the bottom glyph touches. Magnetic tricks are irrelevant because Board has no Hall-sensor grid ([GaussStones](https://graphics.cmlab.csie.ntu.edu.tw/~robin/docs/uist14.pdf)).

## Eight reusable mechanisms cover nearly every planned game

All of these are design inferences built from the documented contact model and from prior art. None has been tested on Board. Mechanisms 1–5 use only official glyph data, so they carry the least risk. Mechanisms 6–8 rely on DIY conductive contacts.

| # | Mechanism | Physical build | What Board senses | Prior art | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Rotating collar / dial** | A fixed outer ring on felt feet holds an inner rotor gripping an official piece. A printed pawl gives detent clicks. Windows in the ring show the state. | Glyph orientation (about 1°). Snap to detents with about 5° tolerance; 1° "reads as broken" ([design guide](https://docs.dev.board.fun/guides/piece-interaction-design)) | ZebraWidgets dials ([CapStones](https://hcie.csail.mit.edu/research/capstones/capstones.html)); HeroClix dial ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HeroClix)) | Low |
| 2 | **Turntable / crank** | A crank handle turns a geared or direct rotor holding the official piece. A ratchet gives Tzolk'in-style clack. | Cumulative rotation and rotation speed | Tzolk'in gear ([Meeple Mountain](https://www.meeplemountain.com/top-six/top-6-irresistibly-touchable-games/)) | Low; watch tracking at high spin speed |
| 3 | **Two-glyph frame (Spycraft-style)** | A printed frame or track holds piece A fixed while piece B slides or rotates in a slot | Distance or angle between A and B gives slider/dial values; it is immune to where the whole assembly sits | Spycraft Spy Kit ([Meeple Mountain](https://www.meeplemountain.com/reviews/board-fun-device/)) | Low-medium; uses two official pieces |
| 4 | **Lift-and-drop selector / plunger** | A housing holds 2–4 official pieces, and a lever lowers one onto the glass. Or a spring plunger lifts one piece a few mm to make a button. | Which glyph is present; Began/Ended phase | Design pattern from [notes on CapStones/Clip-on](https://hcie.csail.mit.edu/research/capstones/capstones.html) | Medium; costs pieces, and Ended latency and persistence settings need testing |
| 5 | **Windowed / light-pipe shell** | Clear resin lens or acrylic fibers offset from the base, or a red-filter decoder window | Nothing optical; the game draws under the window using glyph pose | Transporters ([RWTH](https://hci.rwth-aachen.de/transporter)); Lumino ([HPI](https://hpi.de/baudisch/projects/lumino.html)) | Low-medium; brightness unmeasured |
| 6 | **Jigsaw-keyed parts** | Toppers with puzzle edges so only valid neighbors physically fit | Adjacency and relative angle of the glyphs underneath | Tern blocks made syntax errors "impossible" ([Horn et al.](https://tidal.northwestern.edu/media/files/pubs/idc08.pdf)) | Low (keys are mechanical) |
| 7 | **Stamp** | Either an official base inside a stamp handle, or a DIY conductive constellation | Official: Began at a location. DIY: a cluster of Finger/Blob points decoded TouchTokens-style | Marbotic ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/229501/marbotic-smart-letters-and-smart-numbers-review-toys-tablets-educational-fun.html)); SnowShoe ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2015/04/22/backed-by-3-5-million-snowshoe-debuts-clever-plastic-stamps-that-unlock-content-on-mobile-phones)) | Official: low. DIY: high |
| 8 | **Grounded pad button / conductive tip** | A conductive column from grip to a 10 mm conductive foot, either spring-lifted or always down with a switch, or a stylus-like tip | A Finger contact at a known offset from a nearby glyph | Clip-on Gadgets ([UIST 2011](https://core.ac.uk/download/357520689.pdf)); DuoTouch coded sliders ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.17961)) | Medium (tip) to high (switched pads) |

Mechanisms 1 and 3 carry most of the creative weight, because they turn one official glyph into a continuous control, the way Spycraft does. The turntable variant matters because the shell, not the child's hand, keeps the base flat and centered. That protects tracking. A rotating official base also drags on the glass, so test for scuffing and use rim felt only on the stationary outer ring.

### Recommended path

Buy or request **Board Bases** first and confirm whether they are sold separately; the notes found no answer, so ask hello@board.fun. Build one universal "socket" topper with a press-fit or snap mount that leaves the base underside bare and adds a copper-tape strip from the grip to the base body. Then design every game piece as a swappable shell on that socket. For more identities, layer in the owned retail sets: Arcade, Chop Chop and Heist, plus the combined models. Keep DIY conductive work to a single experimental piece (the Circuit Maker iron) until the tests below pass. In parallel, design a pitch-ready custom set in the Unity simulator, which lets you define icons for any glyph ID ([Unity Simulator](https://docs.dev.board.fun/unity/simulator)), and watch for Board Studio.

## Hardware test plan for day one

Run each test in the sandbox contact debugger. Log contact type, glyph ID, x/y, orientation, isTouched and phase at 60 fps, and record pass/fail thresholds. Measure base diameter, height and glyph-zone footprint with calipers before printing anything, because no piece dimensions are published.

| Test | Procedure | Decides |
|---|---|---|
| ID inventory | Place every owned piece one at a time per model and log IDs; also test the combined models | Piece budget per game |
| Untouched persistence | Leave each piece resting 10 min; watch for drops. Then repeat with each persistence setting. | Whether resting toppers stay tracked |
| Touched through shells | Bare piece vs PLA shell vs shell + copper tape vs shell + Proto-pasta path | Hold-to-activate designs (flashlight, lens, planchette) |
| Shell mass/proximity | Shells touching the base's sides; printed caps on top; metal-tape near the glass | Whether toppers confuse the classifier |
| Lift threshold and latency | Shim the base at 0.5/1/2/3 mm and time when Ended fires | Plunger buttons and selectors |
| Rotation quality | Spin at slow, medium and fast speeds; measure jitter at rest and dropouts at speed | Detent tolerance, crank speed caps |
| Two-glyph frame | Two pieces in a slot at 5–60 mm apart; minimum spacing before misreads | Spycraft-style sliders |
| DIY pad size sweep | Conductive feet at 6/8/10/12/15 mm, touched and untouched; record Finger vs Blob vs nothing | Iron tip, stamps, buttons |
| Palm filter ceiling | Pads 20–40 mm, and constellations at 10–25 mm spacing | Upper size limit for DIY contacts |
| Blob behavior | Coins, foil disks, conductive PLA disks; record type, position accuracy, persistence | Whether anonymous tokens are usable |
| Windows and light | Clear resin vs PETG lens offset from the base; check visibility at different viewing angles | Peek Park, beakers, planchette |
| Model switching | Switch Piece Set settings mid-app | A single family launcher app |
| Glass wear | Rotating bases and conductive PLA tips for 500 turns; inspect the glass | Felt and ironing requirements |

## Safety for kids' printed parts

The youngest player is about five, so the CPSC small-parts *ban* (which covers products for under-3s) does not apply. Parts that fit the **1.25" × 2.25" cylinder** still warrant the choking-hazard caution for ages 3–6. Make every detachable bit larger than that cylinder, or keep it captive on a hinge or tether, especially with younger visitors around ([CPSC small parts](https://cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Business-Guidance/Small-Parts-for-Toys-and-Childrens-Products); [ComplianceGate](https://www.compliancegate.com/small-parts-regulations-warnings-united-states/)). Design for abuse. Hinges and detents fatigue, so use walls of 2 mm or more, print-in-place hinges with generous clearance, and TPU for flex parts. Avoid thin cantilevers. Following Nintendo Labo's example, make repair part of play: Labo durability-tested repeated motions thousands of times and shipped replacement materials from launch ([Nintendo Labo interview](https://www.nintendo.com/au/news-and-articles/nintendo-labo-developer-interview-part-2-prototyping-and-development/)). These general norms are not sourced in the notes but are prudent: no loose magnets or button batteries in any topper; no sharp metal on the soldering-iron prop (rounded tip, bright "pretend" colors); fully cure resin prints before kids handle them; deburr or iron surfaces that touch the glass; and keep conductive tape edges sealed so they cannot peel into sharp slivers. Board's guidance not to drop or slam pieces applies doubly to heavy shells ([Game Submissions](https://docs.dev.board.fun/game-submissions)).

## Conclusion

The creative opportunity on Board is mechanical, not electrical. Since recognition is closed, the family's edge is how cleverly printed parts constrain and amplify official glyphs: collars that turn an angle into a clicky dial, frames that turn two pieces into a slider, housings that turn a lift into a button, and offset windows that let the screen glow inside an object. Spycraft has already proved the commercial version of this. A hobbyist kit built around Board Bases could reach Spycraft-level inventiveness across a dozen games without ever creating a new glyph.

The biggest open question is how Board handles non-glyph conductive contacts: untouched visibility, palm filtering and Blob behavior. A single afternoon of debugger tests answers it, and it decides whether Circuit Maker's iron, true stamps and DIY tiles are real or cosmetic. Designing the kit now around the safe mechanisms, and documenting a custom set in the simulator, also prepares a credible pitch if Board opens custom pieces through Studio or a publishing partnership.
